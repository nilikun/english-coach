# Medium: formal writing, email, chat, slides, speech

`register.md` is about **formality**: who the reader is. This file is about **medium**: how the words reach them. A reviewer reads a paper slowly and can re-read. A manager reads email on a phone. A colleague scans chat in seconds. An audience glances at a slide while listening. A listener hears a talk once and can't go back.

The same idea needs different words in each. Learners often know one of these well, usually exam-style written English, and use it everywhere: talks that sound like papers being read aloud, papers with *a lot of* and *get*, slides covered in full sentences.

Load this file when:

- the text is a slide, a talk script, a chat message, or meeting notes;
- the words don't match the medium (spoken words in a paper, paper sentences in a talk);
- the user asks to turn one medium into another ("把这段论文改成讲稿", "スライドにして", "turn this into an email").

## 1. How the five media differ

| Feature | Formal writing (paper, report) | Work email | Chat | Slide | Speech (talk, meeting) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| How it's received | Read closely, re-read | Read quickly, often on a phone | Scanned in seconds | Glanced at while listening | Heard once |
| Sentences | Complete; 15–25 words; subordinate clauses fine | Complete, shorter | Fragments fine | Title: one full sentence. Bullets: fragments, about 8 words or fewer | Short, one idea each; repetition is fine |
| Contractions | No in papers; rare in reports | Yes | Yes | Rarely needed | Yes, always |
| Phrasal verbs | Prefer one-word verbs: *investigate, increase, determine* | Both | Yes | Short ones | Prefer them: *look into, go up, find out* |
| Articles | Always | Always | Often dropped | Usually dropped in bullets, kept in titles | Always |
| Pronouns | *we* (most fields); *I* rarely; no *you* | *I, we, you* | *I, you* | Mostly none | *I, we, you, let's* |
| Linking words | *however, therefore, in addition* | *but, so, also* | *but, so* | Layout does the linking: order, arrows, two columns | *but, so, and*; *Now…*, *OK, so…* |
| Numbers | Exact, with units: *48.3% (n = 120)* | Exact or rounded: *about 48%* | Rounded: *~50%* | Short forms: *−48%*, *2×*, *120 → 62 ms* | Rounded for supporting numbers: *almost half*. The one or two headline results of an academic talk stay exact: *92.3 percent* |
| Symbols, abbreviations | Defined first; no symbols in prose | Few | Many (*FYI, EOD*) | Fine: *→ ≈ × ↑ vs.* | None: say *versus*, *roughly*, *times* |
| Signposting | Light: *Section 3 describes…* | Light: *Two updates:* | None | The slide structure is the signpost | Essential: *First…*, *Let's move on to…*, *The key number here is…* |
| Hedging | Calibrated verbs: *suggests, indicates* | Moderate | Little | None on the slide; put caveats in speaker notes | Spoken hedges are fine: *probably*, *I think*, *my guess is* |
| Questions | Rarely | For requests | Yes | A question as a title can work: *Why does recall drop?* | Rhetorical questions help: *So why does this happen?* |

## 2. Word choices by medium

"Formal" doesn't mean rare. *Utilize, facilitate, commence* read as AI or bureaucracy even in papers (see `de-ai.md`). The formal column below lists the plain words a careful writer uses.

| Function | Formal writing | Email | Slide | Speech |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contrast | *however*; *in contrast* | *but*; *that said* | *vs.*, two columns | *but*; *on the other hand* |
| Addition | *in addition*; *also* | *also* | new bullet | *also*; *on top of that* |
| Cause | *because*; *due to* | *because*; *since* | *due to X*; *←* | *because*; *that's why* |
| Result | *therefore*; *as a result* | *so* | *→* | *so*; *which means* |
| Example | *for example*; *(e.g., X)* | *for example* | *e.g.* | *for example*; *say,* |
| Sequence | *first*, *second*; *then* | *first*, *then* | numbered list | *first*; *next*; *after that*; *finally* |
| Wrap-up | *in summary*; *overall* | *in short* | *Takeaway:* | *so, to sum up*; *the main point is* |
| Approximate | *approximately*; *roughly* | *about*; *around* | *~* | *about*; *almost*; *just under* |
| Increase | *increased*; *rose* | *went up*; *grew* | *↑*, *+38%* | *went up*; *jumped* |
| Get | *obtain*; *receive*; *achieve* | *get* | omit the verb | *get* |
| Find out | *determine*; *identify* | *find out* | — | *find out*; *figure out* |
| Look into | *investigate*; *examine* | *look into* | — | *look into*; *check* |
| Need | *require* | *need* | *Needed:* | *need* |
| Show | *show*; *demonstrate* (strong evidence) | *show* | the chart shows it | *as you can see here* |
| Big / a lot | *large*; *substantial*; *many*; *much* | *big*; *a lot of* | the number itself | *big*; *a lot of*; *huge* (sparingly) |
| Opinion | *we argue*; *this suggests* | *I think* | — | *I think*; *my guess is* |
| Problem | *limitation*; *challenge* | *issue*; *problem* | *Risk:*, *Limitation:* | *the problem is*; *one catch is* |
| Ask | *We would be grateful if…* (letters only) | *Could you…?* | *Next step:*, *Ask:* | *What I need from you is…* |
| Now | *currently*; *at present* | *now*; *currently* | a date | *right now*; *at the moment* |
| Later | *in future work*; *subsequently* (rare) | *later*; *by Friday* | *Next:* | *later*; *after this* |

## 3. The same content in five media

Use these to show the user what changes and why. Adapt the pattern to their content rather than copying the sentences.

**A result**

| Medium | Version |
|---|---|
| Paper | *The proposed method reduced median latency from 120 ms to 62 ms (−48%; Table 3).* |
| Report | *The new method cut median latency by 48%, which meets the Q3 target of 70 ms.* |
| Email | *Good news on latency: the new method brings it down from 120 ms to 62 ms.* |
| Chat | *latency down to 62ms with the new method (was 120)* |
| Slide | Title: *The new method halves latency*. Bullet: *Median latency 120 → 62 ms (−48%)* |
| Speech | *With the new method, latency dropped by almost half, from about 120 milliseconds to about 60.* |

**A limitation**

| Medium | Version |
|---|---|
| Paper | *The method was evaluated only on English text, which limits how far these results generalize.* |
| Report | *Risk: the method has only been tested on English text. We will test it on Japanese data in Q4.* |
| Email | *One caveat: we haven't tested it on non-English text yet.* |
| Chat | *caveat: English only so far* |
| Slide | *Limitation: English-only evaluation* |
| Speech | *One thing to keep in mind: we've only tested this on English so far. Japanese is next.* |

**A request**

| Medium | Version |
|---|---|
| Formal letter | *We would be grateful if you could provide the raw data by 15 November.* |
| Email | *Could you send me the raw data by Friday, 15 November?* |
| Chat | *could you send the raw data by Fri 15th?* |
| Slide | *Next step: raw data from the QA team (due 15 Nov)* |
| Speech | *So what I need from your team is the raw data, ideally by the fifteenth.* |

## 4. Mixing mistakes to flag

Mark these 🗣️ in a review, with the slug and one line saying which medium the word belongs to.

| slug | Mistake | Example → fix |
|---|---|---|
| `spoken-in-writing` | Spoken words in a paper, formal report or work email (fillers and intensifiers such as *So, basically, kind of, super*; in papers also *a lot of, get, big*, contractions): contractions, *a lot of, get, big, kind of, stuff*, *So,* or *OK,* as openers, rhetorical questions, *you* | *We got a big improvement.* → *Accuracy improved substantially (from 81% to 89%).* |
| `written-in-speech` | Paper sentences in a talk: long noun phrases, *furthermore, it can be observed that, respectively*, citations read aloud | *Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure 3, it can be observed that…* → *Now look at Figure 3. You can see that…* |
| `respectively-spoken` | *Respectively* in speech. Listeners can't hold two lists in their head to pair them. | *A and B reached 80% and 90%, respectively.* → *A reached 80%, and B reached 90%.* |
| `citation-spoken` | Reference numbers read aloud | *As shown in [12]…* → *A 2023 study from Google found…* |
| `sentence-on-slide` | Full sentences or paragraphs in slide bullets | *The proposed method achieves the best results on all three datasets.* → *Best on all 3 datasets* |
| `topic-title` | Slide title that names a topic instead of stating the point | *Results* → *The new method halves latency* |
| `slide-in-prose` | Slide shorthand in a report or email: arrows, *w/*, *+38%* without a noun, *approx.* | *Latency ↓ 48% w/ new method.* → *Latency fell 48% with the new method.* |
| `chat-in-email` | Chat style in email: lowercase, no greeting, *pls, thx*, emoji to external readers | *pls check thx* → *Could you check this? Thanks.* |
| `number-medium` | Numbers in the wrong form for the medium: a table of decimals read aloud; *~50%* in a paper | Speech: *almost half* for supporting numbers. Paper: *48.3%*. |
| `nominalization` | Verbs turned into nouns, so a sentence is a chain of *the X of the Y of…*; hard to read and harder to hear | *the effectiveness of the approach depends on the availability of sufficient data* → *the approach needs enough data* |
| `noun-stack` | Stacked nouns, common in slides by Japanese (体言止め) and Chinese writers | *Cost reduction by cache layer introduction* → *Adding a cache cuts cost* |
| `stiff-speech` | Over-polite or bookish spoken English | *I would like to explain about the background.* → *Let me start with some background.* |

Slug families count as one habit for the hand-over rule in `review.md`:

- Paper English in a talk: `written-in-speech`, `respectively-spoken`, `citation-spoken`, `stiff-speech`, `nominalization`
- Slide form: `sentence-on-slide`, `topic-title`, `noun-stack`
- Casual words in writing: `spoken-in-writing`, `chat-in-email`, `slide-in-prose`

### First-language patterns

- **Chinese writers** often learned English through exam essays, so their speech sounds written (`written-in-speech`) and their papers pick up exam-essay connectors (*What's more, Besides, In a word*). Chinese slide culture favors dense text; expect `sentence-on-slide`.
- **Japanese writers** often carry です/ます politeness into speech as *I would like to…* on every sentence (`stiff-speech`), and 体言止め slide bullets into noun stacks (`noun-stack`). In papers, *I think* (と思います) leaks in (`spoken-in-writing`).
- **Singapore writers** usually handle speech well; watch chat style in email (`chat-in-email`) and Singlish in slides shown to overseas audiences.

## 5. Converting between media

When the user asks to turn text from one medium into another, treat it as coaching, not ghostwriting. The content is theirs; the skill is knowing what changes.

1. **List the changes this conversion needs**, 3 to 5 lines, using section 1. Paper → talk, for example: split long sentences; add signposts; round the numbers; say citations in words; drop *respectively*; use *we* and *you*.
2. **Convert the first part as a model**: one paragraph, or one or two slides. Mark each change with the rule it follows.
3. **Hand over**: ask the user to convert the next part with the same rules, then review it.

If the user explicitly asks for the whole conversion ("改成 2 页 slides 和讲稿"), or is in a hurry, convert all of it. An explicit request beats the hand-over default. Then make the lesson land another way: a side-by-side table and a practice step on a new passage.

### Output for a conversion

```markdown
**场景**：… (one line, as in review.md)
⚠️ Anything in the source that is unclear or wrong, first (it will be wrong in every medium).

## [Target medium] version
The converted text: slides with titles and bullets, or the script, plus speaker notes with exact numbers.

## What changed and why
| Source (theirs) | Converted | Rule, with marker and slug |

## The rules for this conversion
3–5 lines the user can reuse next time.

Then the closing items from SKILL.md Step 4 (one offer at most besides the notebook, one next step: convert another passage themselves).
```

Use the markers from `review.md` (❌ ⚠️ 🗣️ 🤖 💡 ➕).

Keep facts and claims unchanged across media. In speech, round supporting numbers and keep the exact figure in the speaker notes; say the headline result of an academic talk exactly. Keep hedges: *suggests* in the paper can become *probably* in speech, not *proves*.
