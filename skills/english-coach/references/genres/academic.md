# Academic writing

Papers, theses, abstracts, grant proposals, reviewer responses, and letters to editors. Load the first-language file too: most academic problems for Chinese and Japanese writers are the habits listed there.

## What each section has to do

Check each section against its job before checking sentences. A grammatically perfect introduction that never states the gap still fails.

| Section | Job | Common problem |
|---|---|---|
| Title | Say what was studied and, ideally, the main finding | Starts with *Research on…* / *Study of…* (关于……的研究). Cut it and name the topic. |
| Abstract | Background (1 sentence) → gap → what you did → main result with a number → implication | Background takes half the abstract; no numbers in results. |
| Introduction | Field → what's known → gap → this study's aim and contribution | *With the development of…* opener; the gap is implied, never stated. |
| Methods | What you did, in enough detail to repeat it | Present tense mixed with past; missing parameters. |
| Results | What you found, with numbers, pointing to figures and tables | Interpretation mixed in; *It can be seen from Fig. 3 that…* |
| Discussion | What the results mean, how they compare with prior work, limitations | Repeats results; no limitations; overclaims. |
| Conclusion | Main finding and its consequence, in a few sentences | Copy of the abstract; *In a word*; new results. |

## Tense conventions

- **Methods and your results**: past. *We measured…*, *The yield increased to 82%.*
- **Established knowledge**: present. *Graphene conducts heat well.*
- **Figures and tables**: present. *Figure 2 shows…*, *Table 1 lists…*
- **Prior studies**: past for a specific study (*Li et al. reported…*), present perfect for a body of work (*Several studies have shown…*).
- **The paper itself**: present. *This paper describes…*, *Section 3 presents…*
- **Abstracts**: present for what the paper does and claims (*We propose…*, *The results show…*), past for specific experiments (*We tested the method on…*). Many engineering abstracts use present throughout; that's acceptable. Don't flag it.

## Claims and hedging

Match the verb to the evidence. This is where reviewers most often push back on non-native authors, in both directions.

| Evidence | Verb |
|---|---|
| Proven, or directly measured | *show*, *demonstrate*, *find* |
| Strong but indirect | *indicate*, *suggest strongly* |
| Moderate | *suggest*, *point to* |
| Weak, speculative | *may*, *might*, *could* |

- Avoid *prove* outside mathematics.
- One hedge per claim. *This may possibly suggest* has three.
- *To our knowledge, this is the first…* is the accepted way to claim novelty. *For the first time in the world* is not.
- *Significant* implies a statistical test. Report the test, or use *substantial*.

## Using "we" and active voice

Most journals in science, engineering, medicine and social science accept *we*. *We measured* is shorter and clearer than *It was measured that*. Keep passive voice when the actor doesn't matter (*Samples were stored at −80 °C*). If the user's target journal forbids *we*, follow it.

## Citations in the sentence

- Choose the reporting verb by what the source did: *reported*, *found*, *proposed*, *argued*, *showed*. Not *pointed out* for everything.
- Don't stack citations as the subject: *[3–9] studied…* → *Several groups have studied… [3–9].*
- *According to [5]* is fine once, tiresome three times in a paragraph.

## Formatting checks (from Brittman and common style guides)

- Spell out a number that starts a sentence, or rephrase.
- Use one style for figure references, the one the journal uses (*Fig. 2* or *Figure 2*).
- Italicize variables in text; keep units roman, with a space: *5 mm*.
- No math symbols in running prose (*greater than*, not *>*).
- Abbreviations: define at first use, then use consistently; don't start a sentence with one.

## Letters to editors and reviewer responses

**Cover letter to the editor**: one paragraph on what you found and why it suits this journal; standard declarations. No flattery (*your esteemed journal*).

**Response to reviewers**: point by point, quoting each comment.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| *We are very sorry for our careless mistake.* | *Thank you for catching this. We have corrected it (p. 5, line 112).* |
| *We totally agree with the reviewer.* (to every comment) | *We agree.* Then what you changed. |
| *The reviewer is wrong.* | *We respectfully disagree, because… To make this clearer, we have added…* |
| *We have revised the manuscript according to your suggestion.* | Say exactly what changed and where. |

Japanese and Chinese authors often over-apologize; keep thanks short and specific, and spend the words on the change.

## Thesis and grant notes

- **Thesis**: the same section logic, scaled up. Chapter introductions say what the chapter does and how it connects; avoid repeating the full thesis aim in each chapter.
- **Grants**: reviewers skim. Put the aim and the expected outcome in the first sentence of each section. Use *will* for planned work, not *would*. Replace *we hope to* with *we will*.

## Emailing a professor

Covers first contact with a prospective supervisor (套磁, 研究室訪問の依頼), questions to your own supervisor, and admissions offices. See `email.md` for structure. Specific to academia:

- *Dear Professor Wang* or *Dear Dr. Wang*. Not *Dear Teacher*, not *Hi* for a first email.
- Say in the first two lines who you are and what you want (*I'm a second-year PhD student in Dr. Li's group, and I'd like to ask whether…*).
- Attach what they need (CV, draft) and name it in the email.
- Show you read their work: name one paper and one specific point. *Your groundbreaking research* tells them nothing and reads as AI.
- Keep it to 150–200 words, readable on a phone.
- Follow up after 7–10 days, once, politely.
