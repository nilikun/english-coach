# Reports, memos and proposals

Workplace documents have one reader problem: busy people read the top and skim the rest. Check that the document survives a 30-second read before polishing sentences.

## Lead with the answer

Put the conclusion, recommendation or decision needed at the top. Military writers call this BLUF (bottom line up front); consultants call it the answer first, then the pyramid of support. Chinese and Japanese drafts often follow the order the work happened in (background → method → findings → conclusion). Show the user the reordered outline.

Test: if the reader only reads the first paragraph, do they know what you want them to know or do?

## Common document types

| Type | Opening should contain | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive summary** | Recommendation, the 2–3 reasons, cost or impact, decision needed | Background takes the first half |
| **Status / weekly report** (周报, 週報) | Overall status (on track / at risk / off track), then done / next / blockers | A diary of activities with no status; *We are working hard on…* |
| **Proposal** | What you propose, what it costs, what it gets the reader | Benefits stated as adjectives (*efficient, scalable*) instead of numbers |
| **Incident report / post-mortem** | What happened, impact, cause, fix, prevention | Blame language; passive voice hiding who decided what |
| **Analysis / research report** | Key finding with the number, then method and detail | Method first; finding buried in paragraph 4 |
| **Meeting minutes** | Decisions and action items (owner, due date) first; discussion after | A transcript |
| **Memo / recommendation** | *I recommend… because…* in the first two lines | *This memo aims to discuss…* |

## Numbers and trends

Business writing lives on numbers. These are where non-native writers make expensive mistakes.

| Point | Example |
|---|---|
| *Increase by* vs. *increase to* | *Revenue increased by 5%* (the change) ≠ *increased to 5 million* (the new value) |
| Percent vs. percentage points | Margin went from 10% to 12%: *rose by 2 percentage points* (or *by 20%*). Not *by 2%*. |
| Comparison words | *year over year (YoY)*, *quarter over quarter (QoQ)*, *compared with Q2* |
| Trend verbs | Up: *rose, grew, climbed, jumped* (sharply). Down: *fell, declined, dropped*. Flat: *held steady, plateaued*. |
| Size words | *slightly, moderately, sharply, significantly*: but give the number. *Sales rose sharply (+38%)*. |
| Approximate | *about, roughly, nearly, just over/under*. Not *around about*. |
| Currency and units | *USD 2.5 million* or *$2.5 million*; *5,000 units*; be consistent |
| Large numbers | *million, billion*; Chinese 万/亿 and Japanese 万/億 need converting: 3亿 = 300 million |

## Recommendations and risk language

- *I recommend* / *We recommend* is direct and normal. *It is suggested that* hides who suggests.
- Risks: *There is a risk that X, which would cause Y. To mitigate this, we will Z.* Name the owner.
- Uncertainty: give a range or a confidence level (*likely*, *unlikely*), not a stack of hedges.

## Headings, bullets and tables

- Headings should say the point, not the topic: *Costs will rise 12% in Q3*, not *Cost Analysis*.
- Bullets for parallel items; sentences for reasoning. A list of fragments with no verbs hides the logic.
- Tables for comparisons of more than three items or more than two attributes.
- Keep formatting proportional. A one-page memo doesn't need five levels of headings.

## Slides text

When the user writes slide text: one message per slide, as a full-sentence title (*Churn fell after the onboarding change*), with supporting points below. Not *Background*, *Analysis*, *Conclusion*.
