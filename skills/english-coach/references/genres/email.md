# Email

Most work email by non-native writers fails on structure and tone, not grammar. The reader can't find the request, or the request sounds like an order, or it's buried under three apologies. Check those first, then sentences.

## The shape of a work email

1. **Subject line**: specific enough to find later. *Request: revised delivery date for PO 4471*, not *Request* or *Hello*.
2. **Greeting**: matched to the reader (below).
3. **First 1–2 sentences: why you're writing and what you need.** This is the most common fix for Chinese and Japanese writers, whose drafts usually build context and ask at the end.
4. **Context**: only what the reader needs to act.
5. **The ask, made concrete**: who does what by when. *Could you confirm by Thursday, 14 March?*
6. **Close**: a short line and sign-off.

If a draft has two unrelated requests, suggest two emails or a numbered list.

## Greetings

| Reader | Greeting |
|---|---|
| Professor, first contact | *Dear Professor Tan,* / *Dear Dr. Tan,* |
| External contact, formal | *Dear Ms. Lim,* / *Dear Mr. Sato,* |
| External contact you've exchanged emails with | *Hi Ms. Lim,* or *Hi Jessica,* if they sign with their first name |
| Colleague | *Hi Jessica,* |
| Group | *Hi all,* / *Hi everyone,* / *Hi team,* |
| Unknown recipient | *Dear Hiring Manager,* / *Dear [Team] team,* (*To whom it may concern* is old-fashioned) |

Use the name the person signs with. *Mr./Ms.* + family name; never *Mr.* + given name. *Ms.* is the safe default for women.

## Closings

*Best regards,* works almost everywhere. *Kind regards,* is slightly warmer (common in the UK and Singapore). *Thanks,* or *Best,* for colleagues. *Sincerely,* for formal letters and applications. One closing line, not three.

## Common email types

For each type, check that the draft does the job listed.

| Type | Must do | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| **Request** | Say what, why, by when | Request at the end; no deadline; *ASAP* |
| **Follow-up / reminder** | Refer to the first email; restate the ask briefly; new deadline | Guilt-tripping (*I still haven't heard back*); a fresh essay |
| **Decline** | Say no clearly in the first or second sentence; give a reason if appropriate; offer an alternative | Japanese-style indirect no that reads as yes (*It may be a bit difficult*) |
| **Apology** | Own it once, say what happened, what you've done, what happens next | Three apologies; no fix |
| **Escalation** | Facts, impact, what you need from the reader, deadline | Emotion; blame; burying the impact |
| **Introduction / cold outreach** | Who you are in one line, why them, a small specific ask | Long self-introduction; vague ask (*I'd love to connect*) |
| **Thank-you** | Specific thanks | Generic gratitude that could go to anyone |
| **Status update** | Done, next, blockers | See `report.md` |
| **Job application / to recruiter** | Role, one or two concrete qualifications, attachments | Copying the CV into the email |

## Tone checks

- **Too blunt** (common for Chinese and Singapore writers): imperatives without a softener. *Send me the file today.* → *Could you send me the file today? I need it for the 3 pm review.* A reason softens more than *please*.
- **Too indirect or apologetic** (common for Japanese writers): *I'm very sorry to bother you, but if it isn't too much trouble, could you possibly…* → *Could you…*
- **Too stiff** (common in Hong Kong, Singapore and Indian office English): see the stiff table in `register.md`.
- **AI-polite**: *I hope this email finds you well*, *Please don't hesitate to reach out*. See `de-ai.md`.

## Length

Most work email should fit on a phone screen without scrolling. If it's longer, check whether the context belongs in an attachment or a meeting. Don't cut information the reader needs to act.

## Chat messages (Slack, Teams)

Shorter than email; no *Dear*, no sign-off. One message with the full ask, not *Hi* … *are you there?* … *I have a question*. Use full sentences when writing to someone senior or outside the team.
